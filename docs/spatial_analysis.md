# Do poor states sit next to poor states?

A diagnostic on the map, not a second poverty map. It asks whether the pattern on
*Where poverty sits* is more than the north–south gradient the atlas already
documents, and it is deliberately unable to say anything about causes.

The reader-facing version of this analysis is **one worksheet** in the workbook — the
cluster map (`Where poverty clusters`), which sits immediately after the poverty map —
plus the rows of the reference tabs that explain it. That is a reduction: a second
surface, the diagnostic scatter (`Do poor states sit next to poor states?`), shipped
until 2026-10-18 and is no longer published (`docs/spatial_preregistration.md`,
Amendment 2; `docs/FIX_LEDGER.md` W1). The test, the numbers and the reading are
unchanged, and the map's hover carries the same four frozen lines the scatter carried.
Those words are frozen in `docs/spatial_preregistration.md`, which records in Amendment 1
why a second surface was added after the result was seen and why its colours are
descriptive rather than a significance map.

## 1. The question

If poverty were dealt out across Nigeria at random, a state's score would tell you
nothing about its neighbours' scores. If poor states cluster, a state's score and
the average score of the states it borders move together. The test is whether that
resemblance is larger than chance — and, the part that matters here, whether it
survives once the fact that **the north is poorer than the south** is accounted for.

That control is the whole point. Latitude correlates with MPI at r = 0.82, and
latitude is itself strongly clustered: any spatially smooth measure will look
"clustered" for that reason alone. Reporting the raw statistic without the control
would be reporting the gradient a second time under a new name. The same trap is
recorded for rainfall in `docs/FIX_LEDGER.md` A2.

## 2. How it was tested (fixed in advance)

Everything in this section was written and committed **before** the analysis code
existed; see `docs/spatial_preregistration.md`, which also discloses the
exploratory values that preceded it. It is a specification freeze, not a blind test.

- **Neighbours.** Queen contiguity, from the raw `data/raw/nga_adm1.geojson` — the
  real borders, not the simplified rings the map draws. Row-standardised. Asserted
  before any statistic: 37 units, one connected component, no unit without a
  neighbour. Rook contiguity and 4-nearest-neighbour weights are pre-declared
  sensitivities; on this map queen and rook give the identical graph.
- **Statistic.** Moran's I, which is the slope of the scatter the sheet draws: each
  state's score against the average of its neighbours'.
- **H2, the hypothesis of interest.** Moran's I of the residuals of `mpi ~ lat`.
- **Uncertainty.** 9,999 permutations of the values across the map, seed
  `20261018`. The permutation p cannot fall below 1/(9,999+1) = 0.0001, so it is
  printed as an inequality at that floor.
- **Multiplicity.** 37 local statistics are computed; Bonferroni is the primary
  correction, Benjamini–Yekutieli the pre-declared sensitivity (valid under the
  dependence spatial statistics induce).
- **Decisions.** Any p < 0.05 counts as detected, for H1, H2 and H3 alike. The
  sensitivities are reported and never decide anything.

## 3. Result

*(Generated by `scripts/09_spatial_diagnostics.py` from `data/processed/spatial_global.csv`. Do not edit by hand.)*

Moran's I of the poverty score 0.798 (p ≤ 0.0001, 9,999 shuffles); after accounting for how far north a state is, 0.244 (p = 0.0095); after latitude, conflict and climate together, 0.108 (p = 0.107). How far north a state is explains 77% of the pattern. 16 of 37 states look neighbourhood-like before correction; 1 (Yobe) stands out after correcting for 37 tests, and 0 with the stricter Benjamini–Yekutieli correction.

### The wording on the sheet (The reading, in plain words)

1. Yes. States with similar poverty scores do tend to share a border.
2. But most of that is simply that the north is poorer than the south: how far north a state sits explains about three quarters of what looks like neighbouring states resembling each other.
3. Think of a height map. Two neighbours are usually at similar heights because the land slopes steadily — not because one hill lifts the next. Take the slope out, and only a little resemblance between neighbours is left.
4. Only 1 of the 37 states stands out on its own, once we allow for having looked at 37 states at once.

## 4. What this does not say

- **Not a cause, and not spread.** Neighbours sharing a border is a fact about the
  map. A state's poverty is not shown to move to its neighbour, and no coefficient
  here is a spillover. `docs/CAUSAL_DECISION.md` governs.
- **No worst neighbourhood.** OPHI's standard errors overlap for all 36 adjacent
  rank pairs, so no state is "the poorest" and no cluster may be named the worst
  (`docs/FIX_LEDGER.md` A1). The local result is reported as a count.
- **The cluster map is not a hotspot map.** All 37 states are coloured by
  neighbourhood type, including the 36 whose local statistic does not survive
  correction. Colour is a description of the pattern, never of significance; see
  Amendment 1 in `docs/spatial_preregistration.md`.
- **Not north vs south.** All 12 states with MPI > 0.20 sit in the two northern
  latitude bands, so "poorest states vs the rest" is the same observation set. The
  gradient is described, not made into a geographic claim.
- **Not about anywhere smaller than a state.** The pipeline holds no
  local-government-area poverty data. Contiguity weights also treat states of very
  different size and population as equal units (the modifiable-areal-unit problem):
  Kano and Bayelsa count the same.
- **Weak by design.** At n = 37 the power floor is around |rho| ≈ 0.4. A null here
  would not be evidence of independence.
